Florida wind mitigation inspection guide

What the wind mitigation form checks, which features tend to matter most, and how to get ready for your inspection.

What a wind mitigation inspection is

A wind mitigation inspection documents the parts of your home that help it hold up in a hurricane. The inspector records them on Florida's Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form (OIR-B1-1802), and your insurance company uses that form to decide which wind-loss credits apply to your policy.

The form was revised in 2026. Inspections done on or after April 1, 2026 use the new version, which adds more ways to credit retrofitted roof-to-wall connections such as clips and wraps.

What the form looks at

  • Building code: which version of the Florida Building Code the home was permitted under.
  • Roof covering: the type of roof and whether permits or product approvals show it meets code.
  • Roof deck attachment: how the plywood or decking is nailed to the trusses. The weakest attachment found is what counts.
  • Roof-to-wall attachment: how the roof is tied to the walls, from toe-nails up to clips and single or double wraps. Again, the weakest connection counts.
  • Roof geometry: hip roofs (sloped on all sides) generally earn more credit than gable roofs.
  • Secondary water resistance: a sealed roof deck under the shingles or tile that keeps water out if the covering blows off. Standard felt or underlayment does not count.
  • Opening protection: shutters, impact windows and doors, and garage doors. Every opening is considered, and the weakest one sets the rating.

Features that usually matter most

Credits vary by insurer, but the biggest differences usually come from roof shape, how the roof is tied to the walls, and whether every opening is protected. One unprotected window or an old garage door can lower the opening protection rating for the whole house.

If you're replacing your roof, ask your roofer about a sealed roof deck and upgraded deck nailing. Adding them during a re-roof is far cheaper than doing it later.

How to get ready

  • Make sure the inspector can get into the attic. Clear the access hatch and the area under it.
  • Find your roof permit, roofing contract or invoice, and any paperwork for shutters, impact windows or garage doors. Product approval numbers help the inspector give you credit.
  • Close shutters you want credited, or have them available to inspect.

How long a report lasts

A completed form is generally accepted for up to five years, as long as nothing material changes on the home. A new roof, new windows or added shutters are good reasons to get a new one, since they may earn more credit.

Questions

How much will I save?

It depends on your home and your insurance company. Ask your agent which credits your carrier offers, then compare that to what the inspection documents.

Do I need a new inspection if I already have one?

Not if it's still within five years and nothing has changed. If you've upgraded the roof, windows or shutters since, a new inspection may earn more credit.

Can I do it with a 4-point?

Yes. Many insurers ask for both on older homes, and booking them together saves time and money.
